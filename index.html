About

I use computational methods to study protein variants and protein-ligand interactions. My main line of work is missense variation in metabolic G protein-coupled receptors: I built a filtering and structural characterization pipeline for the GIP receptor (GIPR) and plan to apply it next to the glucagon receptor. I also use molecular docking and molecular dynamics to test natural compounds against enzyme targets, including dihydrotanshinone I against 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH). Alongside this, I work on bacteriophage genome analysis. I am looking for a thesis-based MSc in computational or structural biology.

Research

Dihydrotanshinone I as a candidate 15-PGDH inhibitor

15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH) breaks down prostaglandin E2, and blocking it is being studied as a way to support tissue repair. I asked whether dihydrotanshinone I, a compound from the roots of Salvia miltiorrhiza, is predicted to bind at the enzyme's inhibitor site.

What I did. I docked dihydrotanshinone I into three experimental structures of 15-PGDH (two X-ray, one cryo-EM) with AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, profiled the interactions with PLIP, and ran three 100 ns molecular dynamics replicas of each complex in GROMACS, 900 ns in total.

What I found. Dihydrotanshinone I docked into the inhibitor site in all three structures and scored better than the co-crystallized inhibitor in two of them. Breaking the scores into their components showed that this advantage comes from its rigidity: it pays no torsional penalty, while the inhibitors make stronger contacts. In every simulation it stayed in continuous contact with the protein and kept its contacts with the inhibitor-site residues. It is a candidate for experimental testing, not a confirmed inhibitor.

How I checked it.

  • Redocked each structure's own co-crystallized inhibitor to validate the docking setup.
  • Repeated the docking with 15 random seeds.
  • Reproduced the docking scores with a separate Vina build on a second operating system.
  • Rescored the poses with a second scoring function (Vinardo).
  • Ran every simulation in triplicate, with software versions pinned and documented.

Missense variants in the GIP receptor

The GIP receptor (GIPR) is a metabolic G protein-coupled receptor and one of the two targets of the diabetes and obesity drug tirzepatide. I asked which naturally occurring missense variants in GIPR are most likely to destabilize the receptor or disrupt its function.

What I did. I collected GIPR missense variants from dbSNP and passed them through sequential filters: PolyPhen-2 (HumDiv), a gnomAD minor allele frequency below 0.005, SIFT 4G, PROVEAN, AlphaMissense, MetaRNN, REVEL and mCSM-membrane pathogenicity. I then applied a membrane-specific stability cutoff (mCSM-membrane ΔΔG ≤ −1.0 kcal/mol) and characterized the remaining variants with DynaMut2, Missense3D-TM, UniProt topology and domain mapping, Protter, phyloP, GERP++, ConSurf and HOPE.

What I found. Fourteen variants passed every filter, all of them in the transmembrane helices. One of them, Q224P, sits at the GIP-binding site, which makes it the strongest candidate for pharmacogenomic follow-up.

How I checked it.

  • Required agreement across seven predictors instead of relying on one.
  • Took the stability cutoff from a published benchmark of stability predictors on membrane proteins.
  • Cross-checked the shortlist with Missense3D-TM, a membrane-specific structural tool, which flagged structural damage for 13 of the 14 variants.
  • Assessed conservation at the DNA level (phyloP, GERP++) and the protein level (ConSurf).
